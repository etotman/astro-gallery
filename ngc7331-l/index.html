NGC 7331 & Stephan's Quintet · 1,200 galaxies

Every small green circle on this frame is a galaxy: about 1,200 of them, counted on two nights of luminance from the C14 at f/2. NGC 7331 is the big spiral; Stephan's Quintet sits at lower left. Each faint smudge is a galaxy of billions of stars, most of them hundreds of millions to billions of light-years away.

Spot it: Stephan's Quintet

Five galaxies in a tight knot, four of them colliding about 290 million light-years away. The bluish one, NGC 7320, is a foreground galaxy only about 40 million light-years away.

Where NGC 7331 & Stephan's Quintet is on the sky

The whole sky, flattened: right ascension runs right to left as on a star chart, declination bottom to top. The shaded band never rises above the horizon from San Diego. The month scale shows when each part of the sky is highest at midnight. Hover a dot for its name, click to open that picture.
  • About 1,200 galaxies in 1.7 square degrees, roughly 700 per square degree.
  • Only about 3 % of them have a catalogue (PGC) entry; the rest are anonymous.
  • That is a patch of sky about eight full Moons in area.
  • Often called the Milky Way's twin for its similar size and structure.
  • Stephan's Quintet, the first compact galaxy group ever found (1877), lies about 290 million light-years away; its bluish member NGC 7320 is a foreground galaxy at roughly NGC 7331's distance.
